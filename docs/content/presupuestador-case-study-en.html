No single step in this flow was done badly — the workshop quoted well. The problem was structural: the knowledge lived in one head, information never got centralized, and every adjustment got resolved from scratch instead of becoming a reusable rule. Those three problems — concentrated knowledge, scattered information and repeated work — are what the different solutions that appeared later went on to address, each from a different angle.

This hypothesis didn't translate into a tidy two-stage plan decided in advance. The problem was too big to tackle all at once, so it got broken down into smaller, verifiable solutions as it was better understood — the quick estimator was one of those solutions, not the first of two phases planned from the start.

Every quotation that passed through the spreadsheet got logged, and the spreadsheet itself kept growing into a reusable materials-and-pricing base — the seed of what would later become V2's library of product templates. That base of quotations —not a business number, but the system's own working material— is what later made it possible to compare the model against the estimator's judgment and adjust the weighting, run after run: same case, two criteria side by side; deviations were never averaged out or ignored, each one got reviewed to understand which variable was missing or misweighted. The goal was always for the model to learn to explain the estimator's judgment, not the other way around.

Restimator V1 started as formulas inside that same spreadsheet and moved from there to its own web interface — it was used, and is still used, as a real tool, not a discarded pilot. Its explicit goal was to reduce unnecessary work before the formal quotation, not to replace it.

The real design work was making explicit a judgment one person had never had to put into words — making it clear enough to write down, discuss and correct. The application was, by comparison, the simplest step once that was solved.

I also learned to distrust the temptation to replace expert judgment with a tidy formula: a model that ignores the workshop's real conditions —the relationship with the client, urgency, machine availability that week— would have been simpler to build, and worse. The system had to learn from the estimator, not correct him from the outside.

Restimator answered its specific question well — "do I continue or not" — but it left the bigger problem in plain sight, one that had already been taking shape in parallel since the MVP's own spreadsheet: quoting, approving, producing and tracking a real job is a whole operations problem, not just an estimation one. That bigger problem is what V2 went on to address.

The pricing engine Restimator uses today wasn't rewritten for V2 — it was decoupled from its interface so it could feed a fuller quotation (products, materials, labor, finishes, services) without losing the logic already validated. It's the same architectural decision from the original MVP — logic separated from surface — applied one level up, on the same base that had been under construction from the start.

Two systems-design decisions hold up the rest of the product: a library of reusable product templates, which turns the workshop's recurring knowledge into reusable, editable starting points instead of starting from zero every time; and a conceptual blueprint reader, which explores speeding up measurement extraction without removing the person's final review and traceability. Both pieces are prototypes — explored and documented design decisions, not shipped functionality.
